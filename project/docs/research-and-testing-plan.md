# Research and usability-testing plan

**The scripted classroom exercise and its results are recorded in [the simulation report](research-simulation.md). This protocol also supports a future live replication.**

## Research objective

Find out how football coaches or student analysts currently review shot locations, what makes a pattern worth reviewing, and whether a shot map paired with a statistics table helps them compare zones more accurately or quickly than the same table alone.

The current prototype uses illustrative data. Do not use the example match as evidence about football performance.

## Participants

Recruit 3–5 adults who have experience reviewing football matches as a coach, analyst, or football student. This is a formative usability sample, not a representative sample. Record each participant with a code (P1, P2 …), role category, and relevant experience; do not publish names or identifying details.

## Ethics and privacy

- Explain the purpose, activities, approximate duration, and voluntary nature of the session before starting.
- Ask permission before recording audio or video; written notes are enough.
- Do not collect sensitive personal information or real team/player performance data.
- Make clear that the prototype data are illustrative and that the test evaluates the interface, not the participant.
- Allow a participant to stop at any time. Store anonymized notes only for the course project.

## Part A: exploratory interview

Ask about recent behavior, not hypothetical feature preferences:

1. Tell me about the last time you reviewed shots from a match.
2. How did you decide which moments or patterns to review?
3. What information did you need, and where did you find it?
4. What was confusing, slow, or easy to misread?
5. How did you decide whether a rate based on a small number of attempts was meaningful?
6. What do you do after choosing a pattern for video review?

Use follow-up prompts such as “What happened next?” and “Can you give me a recent example?” Do not pitch the proposed map before understanding the current workflow.

## Part B: comparative prototype test

Use the same task and equivalent data in both layouts. Counterbalance the order across participants (table first for some, map-plus-table first for others) to reduce learning effects. Keep instructions neutral; do not point to the answer or explain the visual encoding.

### Tasks

1. “Which zone had more attempts? Show me how you decided.”
2. “Which zone has the higher conversion rate? What are the counts behind that rate?”
3. “Would you recommend the outside zone from this example alone? Why or why not?”
4. “Choose one pattern for video review, review the summary, save it, and tell me how you know it was saved.”

### Record for each attempt

| Participant | Layout order | Task | Correct outcome? | Completion time | Misread / hesitation | Help needed? | Participant explanation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Pending | Pending | Pending | Pending | Pending | Pending | Pending |

Record observations separately from interpretation. A pause or wrong answer is an observation; the suspected cause is an interpretation to check.

### Analysis

- Compare accuracy and completion time by layout, showing the raw counts and the number of participants.
- Summarize repeated sticking points and any important difference by participant experience.
- Treat this small study as formative. Do not claim statistical significance or that all coaches will behave the same way.
- Compare results with the proposed success criterion set before testing. Report limitations and open questions even if the criterion is met.

## Deliverables after sessions

Add anonymized notes, a short findings summary, a severity-ranked issue list, and a decision-log entry for each design change. Update the presentation only from collected evidence. Until then, leave the result as “pending.”
